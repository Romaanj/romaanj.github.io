replica · reproduction Nº 02

GPTQ, Reproduced

GPTQ: Accurate Post-Training Quantization for Generative Pre-trained Transformers — Frantar, Ashkboos, Hoefler & Alistarh, ICLR 2023 (arXiv:2210.17323). Read top to bottom: the 13 reading questions, answered in one bold sentence each, with the concepts drawn — and probed on our A100 — in between.

Q1 · Background

Why weights are the bill

Generating text from an LLM is dominated by reading its weights — at small batch the GPU is a memory pump — so shrinking the weights shrinks both the memory bill and, with the right kernel, the runtime.

An LLM's weights are fp16 numbers, two bytes each: 326 GB for GPT-3-scale. Each generated token re-reads essentially all of them once. That is why a weights-only trick can matter at all — and why this paper cares about nothing except making the weights small.

Q2 · Problem

The 4-bit budget

Replace every fp16 weight of a 100B+-parameter model with one of just 16 (4-bit) or 8 (3-bit) values, in one shot, without retraining — and lose almost no accuracy.

Quantization means choosing those few levels and snapping every weight onto them — turning a continuous number line into a lattice. The obvious way is RTN — round to nearest: snap each weight, independently, to its closest level.

Continuous in, lattice out

The whole of "quantization", in one picture — per row of the matrix, a min–max grid is fixed and every weight moves to its nearest level.

The rounding error of one weight is its distance to the nearest level. RTN stops here: 16 independent little errors per row, no questions asked. The whole paper is about what to do with those errors instead of ignoring them.

The same picture, on a real layer probe · OPT-125M layer 5 fc1

Not a schematic: 2.4M real weights from one matrix of the model we quantized, with the actual 4-bit grid that row 0 was assigned.

weight distribution, with row 0's 4-bit grid overlaid — 16 levels must cover all of it

Weights pile up near zero, so the levels near the middle do most of the work and the outer ones are nearly idle — one reason low-bit grids hurt, and the reason later papers (AWQ, SmoothQuant) obsess over outliers.

Q3 · Existing limitations

Rounding breaks; accurate PTQ doesn't scale

RTN survives 4-bit but collapses at 3-bit (perplexity 27.7 → 1,300 on our probe), while the accurate second-order alternative (OBQ) tops out near 100M parameters — three orders of magnitude short of GPT scale.

The reason RTN breaks is not that individual weights move far. It is what a layer computes: y = Wx. What matters is never how much W changed, but how much the output changed — ‖ΔW·x‖ on the inputs x the model actually sees. That reframing is the door the whole paper walks through.

Q4 · Goal

OBQ accuracy, 175B scale

Keep OBQ's error-compensating accuracy but make it run on OPT-175B in ~4 GPU-hours — a ~1000× scaling of the same idea, with 3–4-bit weights and near-zero perplexity loss.

Q5 · Method — 1/4 · the playing field

Where this matrix lives

GPTQ walks the network one weight matrix at a time, quantizing each column left to right while the not-yet-quantized columns absorb every rounding error — guided only by the input co-firing statistics, H = 2XXᵀ.

Q5 · Method — 2/4 · the core move

The whole trick, with two weights

Take the smallest layer that can show it: one output, two inputs — y = w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ — and let the two inputs be strongly correlated: when x₁ fires, x₂ fires nearly alike. Round w₁ to its grid and every sample's output picks up an error (q₁−w₁)·x₁. But w₂ is still free, and x₂ can imitate x₁ — so shift w₂ by the right amount and it cancels most of that error without touching the rounded q₁. That is error compensation, the entire idea. Everything else in GPTQ is bookkeeping to do this optimally across 768 correlated inputs at once: H = 2XXᵀ records who co-fires with whom, and the paper's Eq. 2 is the closed form of the slider below.

With compensation, without — same rounding, different output

The same tiny layer drawn twice. Left: round w₁ and do nothing. Right: round w₁ the same way, then shift w₂. The rounded value is identical — only the output error differs.

Numbers from the demo below (ρ ≈ 0.93): rounding error δ = −0.22 on w₁ either way; letting w₂ move by +0.205 cuts the output RMS error from 0.225 to 0.087. Nothing about w₁ was "fixed" — the error was re-routed through a correlated input.

The residual you cannot remove is the part of x₁ that x₂ fails to mimic — with correlation ρ, a fraction √(1−ρ²) of the error survives. Uncorrelated inputs ⇒ no compensation possible. This is why the Hessian — pure co-firing statistics, no labels, no gradients — is the only thing GPTQ needs to know about the data.

Q5 · Method — 3/4 · the bookkeeping

Following the math to the Hessian

Where did the "optimal step" in the demo come from? Not from a neural network — from four lines of algebra. Walk them; each line is one idea.

Four lines from objective to update rule

argminŴ ‖WX − ŴX‖²
The objective (Eq. 1). Only the layer OUTPUT counts — X is the calibration activations from Q5·1/4. This is Q3's reframing, made formal.
E(Δw) = Δwᵀ H Δw,  H = 2XXᵀ
Expand one row (Δw = that row's change). All the data collapses into H — the co-firing table. H is the RULER that measures error: moving weight-pairs that co-fire is expensive, orthogonal moves are cheap.
δF = − (wq − quant(wq)) / [H⁻¹]qq · (H⁻¹):,q
Lock weight q onto its grid, then minimize E over the still-free weights: calculus gives this closed form (Eq. 2). Read it as: "take q's rounding error and distribute it along column q of H⁻¹ — the directions the data can absorb".
Δw₂ = −δ₁ · H₁₂ / H₂₂
The 2-weight special case — exactly the "optimal step" the slider above lands on. H₁₂/H₂₂ = "how well x₂ mimics x₁". No correlation, no compensation.

One more mechanical step in the paper: repeatedly deleting row q from H⁻¹ (Eq. 3) is numerically fragile at scale, and it turns out all the needed rows can be precomputed at once as a Cholesky factor — that is the only reason "Cholesky" appears in the algorithm below.

What H actually looks like

Left: the 16×16 H of the toy layer below (same synthetic inputs). Right: the Hessian diagonal of a real OPT-125M layer from our probe run.

toy H — band = neighbors co-fire
real H diagonal, 768 inputs (log) — from artifacts

The band structure of the toy H is why compensation in the stepper smears into neighboring columns. The real diagonal spans five orders of magnitude: some inputs are enormously more expensive to get wrong — H knows which.

So much for where H comes from. The question that actually decides the algorithm is how it gets used, thousands of times, at run time. The answer is smaller than the derivation: H is inverted and Cholesky-factored once, and after that each column just reads one row of numbers off the factor and adds a multiple of its own error to the columns on its right.

One cycle, with the actual arithmetic

Four weights of one row, a 4-level grid, and the real U = chol(H⁻¹) of this toy layer. Step through: watch a column round, spill its error to the right, and then watch the NEXT column get quantized at its new value — not its original one.

column 0/4

Three things to notice. The only thing taken from H is one row of U per column — everything else was precomputed before the loop started. The spill is strictly rightward, so a locked column never moves again. And each column is quantized at the value it has after receiving everyone else's error, which is why the same weights end up on different grid levels than plain rounding would pick.

Q6 · Core idea

Scaling it 1000×: three fixes

The differentiator is not compensation itself (that is OBQ) but three scaling moves: one shared column order for all rows, lazily batched updates, and a one-shot Cholesky in place of thousands of fragile inverse downdates.

The compensation idea is from 2022's OBQ — which already did it optimally, but kept separate Hessian bookkeeping per row and a greedy per-row order: O(d_row·d_col³), an hour for 25M parameters, hopeless at 175B. GPTQ's three moves, each forced by one observation: (1) order barely matters → use one shared column order for all rows, so the Hessian bookkeeping is shared — three orders of magnitude less of it (its fine print is Q11); (2) column-at-a-time global updates starve the GPU → lazily batch them in 128-column blocks; (3) thousands of repeated downdates of H⁻¹ turn numerically toxic at billion scale → precompute every needed row up front as one Cholesky factor, plus 1% damping. Result: OPT-175B in ~4 GPU-hours — and our OPT-125M in 30 seconds.

Counter-intuitive until you remember Q3: GPTQ deliberately parks weights further from their original values, because the quantity being minimized is the layer's OUTPUT error, not the weight error. A method judged by ‖W−Ŵ‖ would call this worse; the model disagrees.

Q7 · Validation

How the paper checks itself

The paper's yardstick is perplexity on WikiText2/PTB/C4 (plus zero-shot tasks) across the OPT and BLOOM families, against an FP16 baseline and RTN; our GPU runs are probes of the same protocol at OPT-125M scale — there to pull real numbers and tensors into this page, not to re-prove the paper.

Every measured figure below carries its artifact name; the runner was verified against the authors' unmodified code (0.004% ppl difference), and calibration follows the paper exactly — 128 random 2048-token C4 excerpts the model has never been tuned on.

Q8 · Results

What the paper measured

Across the whole OPT family, 4-bit GPTQ tracks the FP16 baseline within a fraction of a perplexity point where RTN drifts or breaks, and at 3-bit RTN collapses by orders of magnitude while GPTQ stays usable — with the gap closing as models grow.

Two readings. Vertically: at every size, compensation beats rounding — and the 3-bit pair is a different universe, not a different score. Horizontally: bigger models are EASIER to quantize (GPTQ-3bit at 175B loses 0.34 ppl; at 125M it loses 26). The one bump against the trend, OPT-66B, is the paper's own footnote: that checkpoint has many dead units in early layers.

The speedup is not from faster math — the kernel dequantizes on the fly and the arithmetic stays fp16. It comes from reading 3 bits per weight instead of 16, which is why the cheaper card (A6000, less bandwidth to spare) gains more than the A100. Quantizing all 175B parameters took ~4 GPU-hours, once.

Q9 · Comparison

Against what, exactly

At GPT scale the only baseline that even runs is RTN — GPTQ beats it by whole regimes (3-bit: 54.7 vs 1,277 on our probe); at small scale it ties the accurate-but-slow school (AdaRound, BRECQ, OBQ) while finishing in seconds instead of hours.

Q10 · Significance

What it unlocked

3-bit OPT-175B fits on ONE A100 (down from five), the dequant kernel turns saved bytes into 3.25–4.5× faster generation, and the recipe seeded the open-source quantization ecosystem that made local LLMs ordinary.

Q11 · Limitations

What it does not do

The speedup is memory-traffic only — no low-bit arithmetic, so compute-bound batch inference gains nothing — activations stay fp16, small models still lose real accuracy at 3-bit, and the "any column order works" licence holds for wide layers, not narrow ones.

That last point is worth pausing on, because the paper hedges it in one clause: order is free "in particular on large, heavily-parametrized layers". On a narrow layer the pool of still-free columns runs out fast, so a bad order leaves late columns with no one to absorb their error. It is the same intuition as the two-weight demo, played to the end: the last column has no helper at all.

Q12 · Future work

Where the thread goes

The paper points to activation quantization and finer grouping; history answered with activation-outlier methods — SmoothQuant and AWQ, the next papers in this series — and later with 2-bit vector quantizers built on the same Hessian machinery.
