DFlash, Reproduced

DFlash: Block Diffusion for Flash Speculative Decoding — Chen, Liang & Liu, ICML 2026 (arXiv:2602.06036). Read top to bottom: the 13 reading questions, answered in one bold sentence each, with the mechanism drawn — and probed on one A100 — in between.

Q1 · Background

One forward pass, one token

An autoregressive LLM must push its entire stack of weights through the GPU to produce a single token, and cannot start the next one until this one exists — so latency is token count × one pass, and that pass is spent mostly moving weights, not computing.

That last part is the opening the whole field walks through. At batch size 1 the GPU reads ~16 GB of weights to do a handful of multiplications per weight; the arithmetic units idle. If you could check several candidate tokens in one pass instead of producing one, the extra ones would come nearly free.

The clock everything is measured against probe · A100

Eight tokens of ordinary decoding from Qwen3-8B, drawn to scale in time. Every box is one full forward pass through the model.

We measured one pass at 27.3 ms on our A100. Nothing in this paper makes that box narrower — the entire method is about getting more than one token out of a box like it.

Q2 · Problem

The sequential bottleneck, now worse

Cut generation latency without changing a single output token — a constraint that turned urgent once reasoning models began emitting thousands of chain-of-thought tokens before answering.

"Without changing a single output token" is the strict part. Sampling a smaller model is fast and wrong; distillation changes the model. Speculative decoding is the one family that promises the identical distribution: guess ahead, then let the real model audit the guesses in a single pass and discard whatever it disagrees with.

Q3 · Existing limitations

The drafter is itself autoregressive

Every strong speculative method before this one drafts with a small autoregressive model, so producing γ guesses costs γ sequential passes — which forces the drafter to be tiny (EAGLE-3 uses one transformer layer), which caps how many guesses survive, which caps speedup at 2–3×.

It is a trap with three walls. A deeper drafter guesses better but costs linearly more time. Longer guesses raise the ceiling but cost linearly more time. A shallower drafter is cheap but its guesses stop surviving. The measurement below is that trap drawn to scale.

An autoregressive drafter pays per token, so its cost is a staircase; a diffusion drafter fills the whole block in one pass, so its cost is a single step. The consequence is not "somewhat cheaper" — it is that guess length stops being a cost at all.

Q4 · Goal

A drafter that is both cheap and good

Break the 2–3× ceiling by making drafting cost independent of guess length, then spend the freed budget on drafter depth and on conditioning it with the target's own hidden states — while verification keeps the output provably unchanged.

Q5 · Method — 1/4 · the cycle, and what τ means

One cycle, τ tokens

Each cycle costs one drafter pass plus one target pass — a fixed ~35 ms on our A100, no matter how many tokens are involved — and yields τ committed tokens, so every idea in the paper is an attempt to raise τ without widening that box.

τ (tau) is the number this method lives on, and it is easy to state and hard to feel: the average number of tokens that survive one cycle. The figure makes it physical. τ — tokens committed per cycle 6

Two things this makes obvious. τ = 1 is worse than not speculating at all — you paid for a drafter and still emit one token per cycle. And returns are not linear: 3.2 → 6.4 nearly doubles throughput, while 12 → 16 barely moves it, because the cycle's fixed cost has stopped being the bottleneck.

Where τ actually comes from probe · 6,023 cycles on GSM8K

τ is an average, and averages hide their shape. This is every cycle of a 128-problem run, bucketed by how many tokens it committed.

The spike at the right edge is the whole block surviving; the mass at 1–3 is the drafter being wrong early. τ is the mean of this distribution, so raising it means shifting mass rightward — not making a few lucky cycles luckier.

Q5 · Method — 2/4 · drafting a block in one pass

Block diffusion, in one forward pass

So the drafter must produce 15 tokens in one pass. An autoregressive model structurally cannot: token 3 is a function of token 2. A block-diffusion model can, because it was trained on a different task — fill in masked positions given everything around them, all at once, the way BERT-style models do, but over a block of future tokens.

The drafter's input and output

One anchor token (the last one the target committed) followed by 15 mask slots. One pass. Bidirectional attention inside the block — position 9 may look at position 14 while both are still masks.

This is also why the drafter can afford depth: its cost is one pass regardless of block size, so the paper spends the savings on five layers instead of one — and five layers of drafting still cost a fifth of a single target pass.

Q5 · Method — 3/4 · verification, exactly

How a guess is accepted or killed

Here is the step that makes the output provably unchanged, and it is simpler than it sounds. The target model runs once over the whole block. That single pass produces, at every position, the token the target itself would have generated. Compare position by position; keep the longest prefix that matches; at the first mismatch take the target's own token instead and throw the rest away.

Q5 · Method — 4/4 · why the guesses are good

The target's hidden states, injected as K/V

A five-layer drafter guessing from scratch is not good enough — the paper's own ablation puts it at 2.8–3.7×. What makes DFlash's guesses survive is that the drafter never guesses from scratch: it is fed the target model's internal representation of the context, which already encodes information about tokens the target has not emitted yet. The mechanism is where that signal is injected.

Q6 · Core idea

Parallelism buys depth; K/V injection makes depth pay

Diffusion drafting removes the cost of guessing far ahead, which makes a deep drafter affordable; injecting the target's features into every draft layer's K/V keeps that depth from being wasted — either half alone lands near 3×.

The paper's ablations are the cleanest statement of this, because each one removes exactly one of the two halves.

Q7 · Validation

How the paper checks itself

Two metrics on eight benchmarks across math, code and chat — acceptance length τ and end-to-end speedup over plain autoregressive decoding — for Qwen3 and LLaMA-3.1 targets, in both a research backend (Transformers) and a production one (SGLang, where concurrency matters).

Since the output distribution is unchanged by construction, accuracy is not the question; speed is. The probe figures on this page are our own runs of the same protocol on one A100 — there to put real tokens and real milliseconds into the diagrams, not to re-prove the paper.

Q8 · Results

What the paper measured

On Qwen3-8B with greedy decoding, DFlash runs 4.7–6.1× faster than autoregressive decoding on math and code and about 2.4× faster than EAGLE-3 at a matched drafting budget — with chat (MT-Bench) the notable weak spot at 2.75×.

Speedup by benchmark, Qwen3-8B paper Table 1

Greedy decoding, Transformers backend, H200. Parenthesised numbers are the drafting budget: EAGLE-3 with a 16-token tree matches DFlash's block size; the 60-token tree is EAGLE-3's own preferred setting.

The gap tracks acceptance length: where DFlash gets τ ≈ 7–8 (structured math and code, where the next dozen tokens are predictable) it runs away; on open-ended chat τ falls to 4.2 and so does the speedup. Note also that DFlash at a 16-token budget beats EAGLE-3 at 60 — a bigger search tree costs verification time that acceptance does not repay.

Q9 · Comparison

Against what, exactly

Against plain autoregressive decoding as the 1.0× floor and EAGLE-3 as the state of the art; the other diffusion-drafter papers (DiffuSpec, SpecDiff-2) are argued against rather than benchmarked, because they had no public implementation — they buy acceptance with 7B drafters and pay it back in drafting latency.

The comparison that matters conceptually is PARD, which trains small autoregressive models to imitate parallel generation. Same goal, opposite direction: PARD keeps the drafter architecture and changes its behaviour; DFlash changes the architecture so that parallelism is structural.

Q10 · Significance

What it changes

It moves speculative decoding from "worth about 2×" to "worth about 5×" on the workloads people actually pay for — long reasoning traces and code — and it reframes diffusion language models as specialist drafters rather than failed generalists.

That reframing is the part with legs. Diffusion LLMs have struggled to match autoregressive quality end to end; as drafters they never have to, because verification supplies the quality guarantee. A weak model with a strong auditor is a different product than a weak model alone.

Q11 · Limitations

Where it gives the gain back

The drafter must be trained per target model, open-ended chat gets roughly half the speedup that math and code get, and the whole advantage erodes as serving concurrency rises — because a busy GPU is no longer waiting on memory.

The mechanism is Q1 run backwards. Speculation converts idle arithmetic into free verification; with 32 requests in flight the arithmetic units are no longer idle, so there is less to convert. Speculative decoding is a latency-regime optimisation, and this chart is where that shows.

Q12 · Future work

Where it goes next

The paper's own loose end is adaptive block-size scheduling — its ablation shows a drafter trained at block 16 degrades gracefully when run at block 8, so a server could shrink the block under load; the authors leave the scheduler unbuilt.

We probed that generalisation on our A100: running the released block-16 checkpoint at block 8 on MATH-500 gives τ 5.66 against 7.78 at block 16 — a real cost, but a graceful one, matching the pattern the paper reports for its own ablation model. A follow-up, DFlash 2, has since appeared.

Q13 · Open resources

What you can run

The authors ship code and drafter checkpoints for Qwen3, LLaMA-3.1 and more (z-lab/dflash), with SGLang and vLLM integrations; our probe runs used the public checkpoint unmodified.
