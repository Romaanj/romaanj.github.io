    goal: '병렬 생성의 초안 생성 속도를 대부분 유지하면서 의존성 인식 drafting의 수락 길이 이점을 회복하고, 실제 서빙 부하 하에서 요청별로 검증 비용을 적응시키는 것. 구체적으로는 dependency-aware 품질에 근접한 수락 길이를 거의 parallel 수준의 drafting 비용으로 내는 drafter, 그리고 고정 block 길이가 아니라 시스템 전체 처리량(기대 수락 token 수 × 해당 batch 크기에서의 엔진 steps-per-second)을 최대화하는 검증 정책이 목표다. 최종 목표는 serving 수준에서 정의된다: baseline이 지탱하지 못하는 SLA 티어까지 포함해 production 시스템의 처리량-상호작용성 Pareto frontier를 밖으로 밀어내는 것.'
    method: 'DSpark는 block 내 의존성을 위한 경량 순차 모듈("Markov head")을 결합한 parallel backbone인 반-자기회귀적 drafter를 사용하며, token별 confidence head가 접두사 **생존 확률**을 추정하고 시스템은 측정된 처리량을 최대화하는 요청별 검증 길이를 선택하는 confidence 기반 검증 스케줄링과 결합한다. Markov head는 직전에 샘플링된 token에 조건화해 각 위치의 logits에 편향을 더하는 low-rank 분해 전이 행렬 B=W1W2(rank **r=256**)로, 무거운 backbone은 한 번만 돌고 샘플링만 이를 통해 좌→우로 진행되어 block 길이를 4→16으로 늘려도 전체 라운드 지연이 **0.2–1.3%**만 늘어난다. 빠른 parallel 타이피스트에 값싼 한 단어 되돌아보기 자동교정기를 얹은 셈이다: 타이피스트는 여전히 한 줄을 한 번에 치고, 자동교정기는 각 단어를 바로 앞 단어에 맞춰 고친다. 학습은 cross-entropy, total-variation 분포 매칭 loss, confidence loss를 가중치 **0.1/0.9/1.0**으로 결합하고 지수적으로 감쇠하는 위치 가중치를 쓰며, target 모델은 동결 상태를 유지한다.'
    key_idea: '학습된 confidence head가 각 draft token이 target 검증에서 살아남을 확률을 예측하고(분석적인 total-variation-distance 수락률로 지도학습), 이 확률들을 drafted 시퀀스를 따라 곱하면 접두사 생존 곡선이 나오는데, 스케줄러는 이를 이용해 생존율이 낮은 요청은 검증을 짧게, 높은 요청은 길게 조정한다. 스케줄러가 기대 수락 token 수를 추정하려면 순위가 아니라 절대 확률이 필요한데 raw 점수는 과신 상태(ECE **3–8%**)라, Sequential Temperature Scaling으로 사후 보정해 ECE를 약 **1%**까지 낮춘다. Hardware-aware scheduler는 모든 요청의 후보 접두사 확장을 생존 확률 순으로 정렬해 측정 처리량(기대 수락 수 × steps-per-second)이 증가하는 동안만 탐욕적으로 승인한다 — 탑승 확률 순으로 standby 승객을 태우다가, 한 명 더 태우는 순간 이미 탄 모두의 비행이 늦어지면 바로 멈추는 탑승 게이트와 같다.'
    validation: 'Qwen3-4B/8B/14B와 Gemma4-12B에서 autoregressive(Eagle3) 및 parallel(DFlash) drafter와 여러 벤치마크 도메인에 걸쳐 오프라인으로 비교했고, DeepSeek-V4 실서비스(V4-Flash, V4-Pro)에 배포해 실사용자 트래픽 하에서 production MTP-1 베이스라인과 비교했다. 모든 drafter가 동일한 학습 레시피를 공유한다 — 1.3M 샘플 Open-PerfectBlend 믹스(math 39.4%, code 38.9%, chat 17.6%)의 prompt만 쓰고 응답은 각 target 모델이 재생성 — 10 epoch 학습.   en:
    background: 'Speculative decoding accelerates LLM inference by having a lightweight drafter propose multiple tokens that the target model verifies in one pass, and recent parallel (non-autoregressive) drafters can propose long sequences in a single forward call. In the DFlash-style design DSpark builds on, the drafter takes one anchor token plus mask tokens and fills the whole block in a single forward pass, with hidden states from selected target-model layers injected as context (KV injection). Because drafting latency is nearly independent of block size, parallel drafters can afford deeper networks under the same latency budget, which buys them noticeably higher first-position acceptance than autoregressive Eagle3 (**0.88 vs 0.81** on Math).'
    problem: 'Parallel drafters suffer rapid acceptance decay toward the end of a drafted block because they lack inter-token dependency modeling, and verifying an entire long block regardless of this decay wastes verification and batch capacity on tokens likely to be rejected. The mechanism is that each parallel position marginalizes over all possible prior tokens instead of conditioning on the exact sampled prefix, so the drafter stitches together inconsistent suffixes: on Qwen3-4B, the conditional acceptance of DFlash falls from **0.87 to 0.78** on Code and **0.72 to 0.63** on Chat between draft positions 1 and 7. It is like a team filling in the blanks of one sentence simultaneously without seeing each other''s answers — every word is plausible on its own, but the sentence they jointly produce often falls apart.'
    prior_limits: 'Purely autoregressive drafters (Eagle-style) model inter-token dependency and keep acceptance stable across a block but are slower to draft; purely parallel drafters draft fast but decay quickly within a block, and neither adapts verification length to the actual accept-likelihood of a request. In fact the conditional acceptance of Eagle3 rises deeper into the block (**0.53 → 0.74** on Chat) because every token conditions on the sampled prefix, but each drafted token costs one sequential forward pass. Existing threshold-based verification heuristics only exploit the rank order of confidence scores; they provide neither calibrated absolute acceptance probabilities nor any coupling to the batch-throughput profile of the serving engine, so verification length stays blind to system load.'
    goal: 'Recover the accepted-length benefits of dependency-aware drafting while retaining most of the drafting speed of parallel generation, and adapt verification cost per request under production serving load. Concretely, the target is a drafter whose accepted length approaches dependency-aware quality at near-parallel drafting cost, plus a verification policy that maximizes system-wide throughput (expected accepted tokens × engine steps-per-second at the chosen batch size) rather than any fixed block length. The end goal is framed at the serving level: shift the throughput-vs-interactivity Pareto frontier of a production system, including SLA tiers the baseline cannot sustain.'
    method: 'DSpark uses a semi-autoregressive drafter — a parallel backbone coupled with a lightweight sequential module (a low-rank "Markov head") for intra-block dependency — together with confidence-scheduled verification, where a per-token confidence head estimates prefix **survival probability** and the system picks a per-request verification length that maximizes measured system throughput. The Markov head is a low-rank factorized transition matrix B=W1W2 (rank **r=256**) that biases the logits at each position conditioned on the previously sampled token; sampling runs left-to-right through it while the heavy backbone runs only once, adding just **0.2–1.3%** full-round latency as block length scales from 4 to 16. Think of it as bolting a cheap one-word-lookback autocorrect onto a fast parallel typist: the typist still writes the whole line at once, and the autocorrect fixes each word against its immediate predecessor. Training combines cross-entropy, a total-variation distribution-matching loss, and a confidence loss (weights **0.1/0.9/1.0**) with exponentially decaying position weights, keeping the target model frozen.'
    key_idea: 'A trained confidence head predicts the probability that each draft token will survive target verification (supervised by the analytic total-variation-distance acceptance rate), and multiplying these probabilities along the drafted sequence gives a prefix survival curve that the scheduler uses to cut verification short for low-survival requests and extend it for high-survival ones. Because the scheduler needs absolute probabilities — not just rankings — to estimate expected accepted tokens, and the raw scores are overconfident (ECE **3–8%**), they are post-hoc calibrated with Sequential Temperature Scaling down to roughly **1%** ECE. The hardware-aware scheduler then sorts all candidate prefix extensions across requests by survival probability and greedily admits them while measured throughput (expected accepts × steps-per-second) still increases — like a boarding gate admitting standby passengers in order of their show-up probability, and stopping the moment one more seat would delay the flight for everyone already on board.'
    validation: 'Evaluated offline on Qwen3-4B/8B/14B and Gemma4-12B against autoregressive (Eagle3) and parallel (DFlash) drafters across multiple benchmark domains, and deployed in DeepSeek-V4 production serving (V4-Flash and V4-Pro) under live user traffic against a production MTP-1 baseline. All drafters share one training recipe — prompts from the 1.3M-sample Open-PerfectBlend mix (39.4% math, 38.9% code, 17.6% chat) with responses regenerated by each target model — trained for 10 epochs. Ablations cover drafter depth, proposal length, confidence-threshold sweeps, and calibration reliability, and the production runs use DSpark-5 (max draft length γ=5) on the DeepSeek-V4-Flash and V4-Pro preview engines.'
    results: 'Offline accepted length improves **26.7-30.9%** over Eagle3 and **16.3-18.4%** over DFlash across the tested Qwen3 sizes; in DeepSeek-V4 production, DSpark improves aggregate throughput **51%** (V4-Flash, 80 tok/s/user SLA) and **52%** (V4-Pro, 35 tok/s/user SLA), and accelerates per-user generation **60-85%** at matched throughput levels. The margin over DFlash widens exactly where suffix decay hurts most: **+16/15/18%** accepted length (math/code/chat) at block length 7 grows to **+30/26/22%** at block length 15.     Speculative decoding began as a statistical guarantee — Leviathan et al. and Chen et al. (2023) showed that a cheap drafter plus batched verification accelerates sampling without changing the target distribution — and the history since has largely been an argument about the drafter's shape. One branch kept dependency: the EAGLE line, up through Eagle3, drafts autoregressively at feature level so every token sees the sampled prefix, paying one forward pass per token. The other branch kept speed: Medusa's parallel heads, the MTP heads DeepSeek trained into V3 (the production baseline here), and block-parallel drafters like DFlash fill an entire block in one pass while each position marginalizes over its neighbors. DSpark is written from the fault line between these branches: Eagle3 and DFlash appear as baselines, but they are really its two parents.

    The conceptual move is to stop treating dependency and capacity as a package deal. The old dichotomy made you pay a sequential forward pass per token for dependency, or give dependency up for a deeper drafter. DSpark's semi-autoregressive split — the heavy parallel backbone runs once, and a rank-256 Markov head re-threads left-to-right conditioning through the sampling step — says dependency is nearly free if you are willing to model only its first-order component. The quieter shift is where speculation gets evaluated: acceptance becomes a calibrated survival probability fed to a scheduler, and verification length a per-request decision made against measured engine throughput. The question changes from "how many tokens can my drafter propose?" to "how many are worth verifying, given everyone else sharing this GPU?"

    What it opens most visibly is a reporting standard: aggregate throughput versus per-user tok/s as a Pareto frontier, with strict SLA tiers "unlocked" — a serving-level framing that offline accepted-length tables cannot substitute for. The tensions it leaves are just as instructive. Drafting cost is paid before anyone knows whether the block will be accepted, so hard queries burn compute the scheduler can never recover — the authors' own future-work pointer, difficulty-aware early exiting, targets exactly this spot. And the deployed scheduler is an approximation of the published algorithm: it sizes verification from confidence signals two steps stale to coexist with CUDA-graph replay, and its throughput model assumes contexts far short of the million-token regime.

    The next moves follow from these seams. A first-order Markov head is the opening bid of a family — the field will probe how much intra-block dependency is enough before the parallel latency advantage erodes. Calibration is promoted from an evaluation nicety to a load-bearing serving component, inviting work on keeping it honest under domain and load shift. And once drafter, confidence head, and scheduler are trained and tuned against one engine, speculative decoding is drifting from an algorithm into a systems co-design problem — one whose progress will increasingly be measured in flight logs, not wind tunnels.
