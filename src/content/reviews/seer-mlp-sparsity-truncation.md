  en:
    background: 'Diffusion multimodal LLMs (DMLLMs) generate a response by denoising an entire fixed-length sequence in parallel rather than emitting tokens one at a time, so unlike autoregressive models they cannot simply stop once an end-of-text token appears mid-schedule. Because true answer lengths vary widely across tasks and are unknown before generation — the paper reports the longest ground-truth answer in a benchmark can be **10x** the shortest — every sequence is padded out to a conservative maximum window, and every one of those padding positions still receives a full forward pass through the model''s layers at every denoising step.'
    problem: 'Padding a DMLLM''s output to a fixed window wastes most of the compute spent per example, since a short valid answer is followed by what the paper calls "a massive sequence of redundant padding tokens" that are still denoised at every step. This waste compounds in multimodal settings because each redundant text position performs dense cross-modal attention against a large visual context, and the paper further argues these padding tokens actively hurt accuracy: through bidirectional attention they act as weak "relay nodes" that leak irrelevant background visual signal back into the valid answer tokens.'
    prior_limits: 'Existing DMLLM acceleration methods largely attack only the visual half of the sequence — approaches such as D3ToM and RedVTP borrow token-merging/pruning ideas from autoregressive vision-language models to compress image tokens, but completely fail to address the redundant text suffix that a fixed-window diffusion schedule still generates. The alternative — EOS-guided early stopping or per-step token eviction — does target the text suffix, but changing the sequence length at every denoising step shatters the static computation graphs (e.g., CUDA Graphs) that inference engines rely on for speed, trading FLOPs saved for CPU launch overhead.'
    goal: 'The goal is to predict, before any tokens are generated, how long a DMLLM''s true answer will be, so the redundant padding suffix can be cut once and the shortened sequence can still be executed efficiently inside a batched serving system without breaking static execution graphs.'
    method: 'Seer is a training-free framework that reads each token position''s MLP activation sparsity at the very first denoising step (step zero) and looks for the position where sparsity jumps sharply — the predicted end-of-text — followed by a stable, high-sparsity "padding plateau"; a signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR) test, comparing a jump-magnitude threshold **τ_jump** against an intra-window fluctuation bound **γ·Δjump**, locates this boundary more robustly than a brittle fixed threshold would. Once the boundary is found, Seer performs a single "macro-truncation": the redundant suffix is permanently removed from the computation graph for the rest of denoising, avoiding the graph-shattering effect of per-step eviction. For batched serving, a hybrid router computes a per-batch Padding-Waste-Ratio (PWR) and dispatches requests to one of three execution paths — a pre-captured static CUDA-Graph path when padding waste is low, a bucketed variable-length graph path for moderate dispersion, or eager execution for stragglers — with all the bookkeeping (prefix slicing, repacking, mask rewriting) done in one fused, device-resident Triton kernel that avoids CPU-GPU synchronization stalls.'
    key_idea: 'The core insight is that a DMLLM already "knows" its true semantic boundary at step zero, before any denoising has meaningfully changed the tokens — a previously undocumented MLP-sparsity signature separates dense-activation answer tokens from a flat, sparse padding plateau even at the very first step. It is like a doctor reading a single early blood marker to estimate how long an illness will run its course, rather than checking in on the patient every day: one cheap, early read of an internal signal substitutes for watching the entire generation unfold.'
    validation: 'The method is evaluated on three DMLLM backbones (LaViDa-LLaDA, LaViDa-Dream, MMaDA) across nine benchmarks (MME, MMMU, MMBench, InfoVQA, ChartQA, ScienceQA, DocVQA, GQA, MathVista) against both DMLLM-specific baselines (D3ToM, RedVTP) and autoregressive-style token-compression methods adapted to diffusion (VisionZip, MMTok, DivPrune, SparseVLM, Focus, Daedal). Separate ablations test the SNR boundary detector''s accuracy (mean absolute truncation error of **0.40-1.18** tokens, over-truncation rate **0.8-3.8%**) and the hybrid router''s batched-serving behavior at batch sizes up to 256.'
    results: 'Reported throughput gains reach up to roughly **31x** (e.g., MMaDA on InfoVQA: 0.13 to 4.02 tokens/s), with per-setting examples such as **7.75x** on MMBench and **10.5x** on MathVista for LaViDa-LLaDA. Accuracy is generally preserved and on some benchmarks slightly improves — DocVQA rises from 63.52 to 63.66 and MMaDA''s InfoVQA accuracy from 15.11 to 15.29 — which the authors attribute to removing the noise-leakage pathway described above; at batch size 256 the full hybrid system reaches 35.84 tokens/s, a **1.6x** improvement over the vanilla baseline, while naive truncation without the hybrid router shows little batched-serving gain.'
    comparison: 'Compared to visual-token-compression baselines (D3ToM, RedVTP, VisionZip, and others), which only shrink the image side of the sequence, Seer targets an orthogonal and previously unaddressed source of waste — the generated-text padding — making the two approaches plausibly complementary rather than competing. Compared to EOS-guided or per-step eviction methods, Seer''s one-shot truncation plus hybrid CUDA-graph/variable-length routing is what actually converts the savings into wall-clock throughput inside a real batched-serving engine, rather than just reducing FLOPs on paper; from an efficient-inference standpoint, the hybrid router and fused Triton kernel are effectively a serving-systems contribution layered on top of the sparsity-detection idea.'

    Seer continues the line of training-free diffusion-LLM acceleration work such as Fast-dLLM, which added a KV cache and confidence-gated parallel decoding to text-only diffusion LMs, but it departs from the dominant strategy for diffusion multimodal LLMs, which has mostly borrowed visual-token-compression ideas from autoregressive VLMs (token-merging/pruning methods like D3ToM and RedVTP). It also sits alongside — and argues against — the variable-length family of ideas (EOS-guided early stopping, per-step token eviction) that tries to shrink the fixed generation window but pays for it with broken CUDA graphs during batched serving.

    The conceptual move is to stop treating "how long will the answer be" as something you can only learn by decoding, and instead read it off an internal signal — MLP activation sparsity — at the very first denoising step, before the schedule has done any real work. Paired with a hybrid CUDA-graph/variable-length serving router, this reframes the padding problem from a per-step, algorithm-level eviction decision into a one-shot, systems-level bucketing decision — trading a small amount of prediction risk (occasional under- or over-truncation) for the execution regularity a real serving engine can actually exploit.

    If a cheap early-layer signal can predict a high-level generation property this reliably at step zero, it raises the question of what else might be legible this early — task difficulty, likely visual-grounding failure, or where hallucination is likely — before most of the compute is even spent. It also leaves open, as the authors themselves note, how far the same MLP-sparsity boundary generalizes across diffusion-LLM architectures and training recipes beyond the three backbones tested here, and whether the same step-zero-read-then-truncate pattern is useful for other fixed-schedule generative models outside the multimodal setting.
