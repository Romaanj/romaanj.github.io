  en:
    background: 'Autoregressive decoding in large language models is heavily memory-bandwidth-bound because every step requires a full pass through the model, and speculative decoding (SD) is a widely used acceleration technique that uses a cheap draft mechanism to propose candidate tokens which the target model then verifies in one pass. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have become a mainstream way to scale parameters into the hundreds of billions while keeping computation in check, by activating only a subset of experts per token.'
    problem: 'Existing SD draft-selection strategies optimize acceptance likelihood alone, but in large-scale MoE models, which draft tokens get selected also determines the union of experts that must be activated during verification. When high-confidence draft tokens scatter across disjoint experts ("expert scattering"), the HBM traffic needed to load those experts grows and eats into the speedup speculation was supposed to deliver.'
    prior_limits: 'Prior draft-tree selection work is largely designed with dense models in mind, maximizing acceptance length without accounting for MoE''s non-uniform memory-cost structure -- that verification cost varies sharply depending on which set of experts gets touched. Given that loading a single expert for DeepSeek-V3.1 (FP8) alone costs 44.04MB of HBM traffic, each mispredicted expert path is a real, easily overlooked I/O tax.'
    goal: 'Reduce the expert-activation cost (number of distinct experts touched) incurred during verification while preserving acceptance likelihood, without modifying the target model''s verification rule at all.'
    method: 'A lightweight expert-activation predictor forecasts, without running the full target model, which experts a candidate draft token would activate, while a dynamic expert buffer tracks which experts are already loaded for the current verification batch. The draft-tree score combines acceptance probability with a marginal expert-cost penalty that falls when a token reuses an already-loaded expert, layered on top of an existing drafter (Eagle-3 or native MTP) so neither the drafter nor the verification rule needs to change.'
    key_idea: 'The central move is optimizing draft selection along two axes at once -- acceptance probability and whether a token reuses an already-loaded expert -- rather than acceptance probability alone, similar to a mail carrier planning a route that favors houses already along the path being walked rather than just the houses most likely to have someone home. This keeps acceptance length nearly unchanged while reducing how many distinct experts must actually be loaded per verification step.'
    validation: 'Evaluated on three real, large-scale open MoE models -- DeepSeek-V3.1 (671B total/37B active, Top-8, MTP-based), Qwen3-235B-A22B (235B/22B, Top-8, Eagle-3-based), and GPT-OSS-120B (120B/5.1B, Top-4, Eagle-3-based) -- across seven benchmarks (GSM8K, HumanEval, MMStar, AIME-25, MATH500, AMC22-24, MTBench) under both greedy and T=1 sampling decoding. Predictor-accuracy sensitivity, an oracle expert-set upper bound, and batch-size scaling are each analyzed separately in the appendix.'
    results: 'On Qwen3-235B-A22B greedy decoding, the underlying Eagle-3 drafter alone reaches 1.22x average speedup with 23.7 average active experts per verification step, while EcoSpec reaches 1.36x with only 20.5 active experts -- essentially the same acceptance length (2.32 vs. 2.41 tokens) achieved by touching fewer distinct experts. The single best benchmark result is 1.62x on Qwen3/MTBench; gains are smaller but still consistent on DeepSeek-V3.1, whose MTP drafter is already strong (1.10-1.28x baseline vs. 1.15-1.33x with EcoSpec).'
    comparison: 'Because this is a selection layer added on top of an existing drafter (Eagle-3 or MTP) rather than a new draft mechanism, it operates at a different layer of the stack than work that redesigns drafting itself. The size of the gain is backbone- and router-dependent, smallest on DeepSeek-V3.1''s MTP (which appears to already route more cooperatively) and largest on Qwen3-235B-A22B.'
    significance: 'For efficient serving, this shows that any other MoE-inference acceleration technique -- quantization, caching, speculative decoding itself -- can quietly give back potential bandwidth savings if it does not explicitly account for which experts a given selection forces to be loaded together. Reframing draft selection as a joint acceptance-and-resource-cost problem places it in the same structural family as other budget-constrained allocation problems, such as bit-width or rank allocation under quantization.'
    limitations: 'No dedicated "Limitations" section was found in the extracted text -- (reviewer judgment) the method requires training a separate expert-activation predictor per target model, adding an offline cost and a dependence on predictor accuracy that the appendix examines but the main text does not foreground. (Reviewer judgment) The paper reports that gains are "backbone-dependent," smaller on the already-strong DeepSeek-V3.1/MTP drafter, without mechanistically explaining why MTP exhausts this particular benefit sooner than Eagle-3-based drafting.'
    future_work: 'No explicit "future work" section was found in the main text -- the conclusion summarizes results across the three models and closes on the general lesson that accounting for expert-activation cost matters when applying speculative decoding to large sparse models. The appendix''s oracle expert-set analysis suggests headroom remains, but the paper does not name concrete next steps to close that gap.'

  en: |-
    Speculative decoding runs from the original draft-then-verify framing of Leviathan and Chen through the draft-tree extensions of Medusa and Eagle, and more recently to multi-token-prediction (MTP) heads built directly into the target model. Almost without exception, this line of work has optimized a single axis -- how likely a given draft token is to be accepted -- which was a reasonable simplification for dense models.

    This paper's conceptual shift is to notice that this single-axis assumption quietly breaks in a Mixture-of-Experts setting -- two draft tokens with identical confidence can route to an already-loaded expert or to an entirely new one, and choosing the latter grows the memory traffic of verification itself, giving back a real share of what speculation was supposed to win. EcoSpec names this "expert scattering" and re-optimizes draft-tree selection along a second axis, marginal expert cost, alongside acceptance probability -- crucially, without touching the drafter itself or the target model's verification rule, adding purely a selection layer on top.

    The question this paper opens is how this "resource-aware selection" principle composes with the other axes of speculative decoding -- for instance, how the expert-cost penalty should change when combined with KV-cache compression or quantized expert weights, or whether the same "reuse what is already loaded" principle could extend to non-linear, block-diffusion-style draft proposals (not addressed in the paper) -- both left as open questions beyond this paper's scope.

## Notes

The paper reports gains that are explicitly backbone-dependent -- largest on Qwen3-235B-A22B, smallest on DeepSeek-V3.1's own MTP drafter -- without offering a mechanistic account of why MTP saturates the benefit sooner. That gap (a reported pattern without a causal explanation) is the most interesting loose thread left by the paper on its own terms.
