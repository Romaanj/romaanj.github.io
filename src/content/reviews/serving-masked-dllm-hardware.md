    background: 'Masked diffusion language models (dLLMs) can in principle generate text faster than autoregressive (AR) models, since they denoise many tokens at once. Recent systems have begun building serving infrastructure for dLLMs, but none had first measured how these models behave under real, concurrent serving load.'
    problem: 'Serving systems built without that grounding risk carrying over assumptions from AR serving that may not hold for dLLMs. This paper closes the gap directly, characterizing LLaDA-8B-Instruct with a D2F (Discrete Diffusion Forcing) LoRA adapter on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU, evaluated on GSM8K and HumanEval.'
    prior_limits: 'Most prior dLLM acceleration work targets single-request latency through caching (dKV-Cache, Fast-dLLM, dLLM-Cache), sparse caches (Sparse-dLLM), or speculative decoding (Spiffy). Only a small group of systems (e.g. dInfer) reports batched throughput directly, and none of them first measured behavior under real concurrent load the way this paper does.'
    goal: 'Characterize dLLM serving under real concurrent load rather than importing AR-serving assumptions, and turn that characterization into concrete design principles for request-difficulty structure, batching throughput, and scheduling policy.'
    method: 'LLaDA-8B-Instruct plus a D2F LoRA adapter runs on a single NVIDIA H200 GPU, evaluated on GSM8K (tier structure, predictability, batching, scheduling) and HumanEval (block-size invariance and variance structure). Kernel execution time is measured directly with torch.profiler, and the measured batch service-time distribution is validated against a 20,000-request Monte Carlo simulation to derive a batch-timeout scheduling rule.'
    key_idea: 'The central claim is that dLLM serving fundamentally needs parallelism at the level of each denoising step, which changes how admission and eviction interact with an already-shared forward pass compared to AR serving. An apt analogy: AR serving is like a counter that serves each customer individually in sequence, while dLLM serving is more like a call system that summons the entire waiting room at once on every step -- so the unit a scheduler batches over has to be the denoising step, not the token.'
    validation: 'All three findings are backed by direct measurement. Request difficulty (the number of denoising steps needed) is discrete rather than continuous, clustering into 11 fixed levels, and no signal the paper tests predicts the level before generation starts (R-squared = 0.150). The paper separately confirms that benchmarks with generation budgets below 320 tokens understate serving variance by cutting requests off before this 11-level structure produces its latency spread.'
    results: 'A full single-request generation takes **10.28s** wall-clock, of which only **2.511s (24.4%)** is GPU kernel time -- the remaining **75.6%** is CPU-side overhead in the per-block control loop (tracking block state, building attention masks, checking thresholds, synchronizing between forward passes). At batch size 8, synchronized batching reaches **16.0x** the throughput of per-request dispatch, not by improving GPU utilization but by letting the whole batch share one fixed per-step CPU dispatch cost instead of paying it once per request.'
    comparison: 'This differs from what AR serving systems (the vLLM class) assume: batching there is mainly about raising GPU utilization, while here it is about amortizing a fixed per-step dispatch cost. The paper explicitly does not compare against existing dLLM inference engines such as dInfer, framing its own contribution as workload characterization rather than a competing system.'
    significance: 'Finding that dLLM serving is currently bound by CPU dispatch rather than GPU compute suggests tightly coupled CPU-GPU hardware (GH200-class superchips) and dispatch-elimination techniques (such as CUDA graphs) could give dLLM serving an outsized benefit. Redefining the batching unit as the denoising step rather than the token is a structural proposal with implications for how future dLLM serving schedulers should be designed.'
    limitations: 'Paper-stated (Sec. VI): experiments cover one architecture (LLaDA base and D2F) on one GPU, the H200. Task generalization (HumanEval) and configuration generalization (base vs. D2F) were tested separately, not jointly. The scheduling analysis uses only 8 measured batches, and the paper reports that a later 32-batch run shifted the estimated capacity by 24%, so it flags that other single-run numbers in the paper likely carry similar uncertainty. The 11-level tier structure was measured at one threshold setting only (tau_add=0.5, tau_decode=0.9).'
    future_work: 'The authors name testing task and configuration generalization jointly, validating on other hardware, direct comparison against existing dLLM inference engines such as dInfer, confirming quality under batching at sizes 8 and 16, and building a working slot-based scheduler design as explicit future work.'

  en: |-
    dLLM acceleration research has mostly focused on cutting single-request latency. Caching methods such as dKV-Cache, Fast-dLLM, and dLLM-Cache found ways to reuse computation across denoising steps (since diffusion models cannot reuse an AR-style KV cache directly), and Sparse-dLLM cut memory further by evicting low-value cache entries. D2F, which this paper builds on, restructures generation into blocks to make step-to-step reuse possible in the first place. Only a small group of systems, roughly dInfer, reported batched serving throughput directly.

    The shift this paper makes is to change the question from "how do we make one request faster" to "what actually bottlenecks real concurrent load." Instead of proposing another caching or sparsification technique, it profiles real H200 hardware serving GSM8K and HumanEval requests concurrently and asks where the time actually goes. What it finds is a structural problem, not a compute problem: request difficulty clusters into 11 unpredictable levels, 76% of wall-clock time disappears into CPU dispatch rather than GPU computation, and even the reason batching helps differs from AR serving.

    What this opens splits in two directions. One is hardware-facing: tightly coupled CPU-GPU superchips and dispatch-elimination techniques could give dLLM serving an outsized benefit precisely because the bottleneck is dispatch, not compute. The other is scheduler-facing: the structural claim that batching should happen at the denoising-step level rather than the token level raises an open question about how future dLLM serving engines should redesign admission and eviction policy around it.
